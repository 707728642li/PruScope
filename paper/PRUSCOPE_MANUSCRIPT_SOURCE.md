# PruScope: Leakage-Controlled Detection, Counting, and Ordinal Stage Phenotyping of Plum from Microfruit to Visual Maturity

**Authors:** Hui Li

**Affiliations:** Research Institute of Forestry Policy and Information, Chinese Academy of Forestry, Beijing 100091, China

**Corresponding author:** Hui Li, lihui09610@163.com

## Abstract

Detecting small green plum fruit in unstructured canopies is difficult because downsampling removes detail and foliage shares its color. We developed PruScope, a single-view RGB pipeline that localizes fruit across three cross-sectional cohorts and assigns ordered stage scores to detected regions. Its capacity-preserved P2 head restored the loss caused by a naïve high-resolution branch in all 12 seed × domain × size comparisons. Three-seed small-fruit AP50–95 was 0.433 internally and 0.374 externally, compared with 0.412/0.360 for YOLO11m and 0.390/0.221 for RT-DETR-L. An exhaustively reviewed 240-image reference contained 5,151 fruit and exposed the remaining limit: AP50–95 was 0.498 overall and 0.205 for small fruit in the small-green cohort. With no CitDet training or tuning, direct inference reached AP50 0.660 and AR50 0.793 on 9,638 small citrus fruit. The ordered stage head achieved reference-crop macro F1 0.979; on 232 fruit-bearing images, strict detector-to-stage macro F1 was 0.946, while joint correctly staged fruit recall across all reference fruit was 0.459. A validation-frozen offline refinement increased small-object AR50 in all three evaluated domains (+0.013, +0.038, and +0.012) at substantially higher latency. PruScope converts orchard images into fruit locations, visible counts, image-domain size strata, and an ordered developmental index. The framework enables cross-sectional phenotyping at image volumes for which exhaustive manual scoring is impractical.

**Keywords:** plum; orchard vision; small-object detection; ordinal phenotyping; visible-fruit counting; developmental stage

## Introduction

Image-based plant phenotyping has moved from controlled chambers toward field systems that must tolerate clutter, occlusion, variable illumination, and uncontrolled cameras [1]. Fruit localization is an especially demanding field task because target appearance changes with development and because neighboring fruit, leaves, branches, and highlights form repeated patterns. Reviews of orchard vision consistently identify small-object recall, cross-domain generalization, annotation cost, and the gap between benchmark accuracy and deployment-level counts as persistent limitations [2]. Earlier deep fruit detectors established the feasibility of using convolutional networks in orchards [3], while recent green-fruit systems have added feature-pyramid, attention, and lightweight components to improve detection against foliage [4–7]. Field-phenotyping studies have also extended image-based counting across canola, grape, coffee, and cereal systems [24–27], reinforcing the need to report biologically interpretable outputs rather than detector scores alone.

Small green fruit remain a hard operating regime. Downsampling an entire high-resolution canopy image to a fixed detector canvas may reduce a fruit to a few feature cells. Standard feature pyramids improve multiscale representation [8, 9], and sliced inference can restore pixels to small targets [10], but an unconditional local stream increases latency and may duplicate detections at tile boundaries. Recent plum, apple, and thinning-stage fruit detectors address this regime with multiscale fusion, semi-supervision, structure-aware heads, or transformers [30–33]. A general YOLO modification likewise advocates a P2 branch for small objects [35]. Yet merely prepending a high-resolution output can silently reduce the hidden width of every detection branch when head capacity is derived from the first—and now narrowest—input level. A principled microfruit detector therefore needs both high-resolution evidence and preservation of the semantic capacity used for larger fruit.

Reference quality and split integrity are a second bottleneck. Domain-adaptive and efficient labeling approaches can reduce annotation effort [11, 12], while simulation and curated phenomics datasets can broaden training coverage [28, 29]. Faint, occluded, and defocused fruit nevertheless require careful full-image review. Agricultural benchmarking is also vulnerable to correlated burst photographs, repeated derivations of one source image, and reuse of protected images during model development [13]. These issues are particularly consequential when apparent architectural gains are small.

Developmental stage adds a distinct modeling problem. Small-green, medium-green, and visually mature fruit form an ordered set, yet separate detectors would fragment the data and create inconsistent boundaries. Single-image phenology models show that ordinal plant state can be estimated without a temporal sequence [34]. PruScope uses one generic-fruit localizer and an ordinal stage model, sharing localization evidence across cohorts while constraining stage probabilities to their known order. Because the images are cross-sectional and were captured by different operators, the resulting index describes cohort order rather than the trajectory or age of an individual fruit.

Here we present PruScope, a unified RGB pipeline for plum phenotyping from microfruit to visual maturity. Three advances carry the main evidence. First, a P2 branch paired with the Cross-scale Capacity-Preserving Head (CCPH) adds stride-4 evidence without narrowing the inherited P3–P5 detection heads. Second, Global–Local Adaptive Fusion (GLAF) reserves tiled inference for scenes selected by a validation-frozen scale–density rule. Third, the Developmental Continuum Ordinal Head (DCOH) returns three ordered stage probabilities and a continuous cross-cohort index. We also subjected Orchard Scale-Selective Attention (OSSA) to a controlled three-seed test; its lack of stable cross-domain benefit is reported as a negative result. A protocol-locked post-review extension, the Density-Aware microfruit Refinement Tail (DART), provides an offline high-recall option while preserving the direct model's global detections. The evaluation spans human and public external references, size- and density-stratified accuracy, count calibration, paired image bootstrap intervals, computational cost, ordinal controls, and adverse orchard scenes. Figure 1 summarizes the safeguards and model lineages.

## Materials and Methods

### Experimental and technical design

The study used a frozen cross-sectional image collection. A source image was the experimental unit for localization and counting; derived resolutions and repeated training entries were kept within that unit. The detector learned one class, `fruit`, across all stages, and DCOH assigned ordered probabilities to detected or reference fruit crops. Detector thresholds, the GLAF gate, and the count confidence threshold were selected on validation images before their corresponding test evaluations. Figure 1 separates the A2 structural lineage, the A5 attention lineage, the historical A5/GLAF deployment path, DCOH, and the optional post-review DART path while tracing the protected data flow.

### Image cohorts, annotations, and split integrity

The internal collection comprised small-green, medium-green, and visually mature plum images captured in unstructured orchards by different operators. These were operational image-cohort labels fixed before model fitting. The small-green cohort contained uniformly green fruit that generally occupied the smallest image regions; the medium-green cohort contained visibly expanded but still green fruit; the visually mature cohort contained fruit with developed exterior color. Every annotated fruit inherited its source image's cohort label. Fruit diameter, days after flowering, firmness, soluble solids, and destructive maturity traits were unavailable, and individual fruit were not reassigned from model outputs. The labels therefore represent ordered visual cohorts rather than physiological maturity classes. Cultivar labels were unavailable or uncontrolled and were not used.

The detector was fitted to 3,684 training entries containing 44,992 fruit boxes, with human annotations for medium-green and licensed external plum images and conservatively filtered teacher-generated boxes for the remaining training-only cohorts. Validation used 151 images with human boxes. The internal localization test comprised 118 unique medium-green source images with 1,423 human boxes. A separate cross-stage reference contained 240 source images (120 small-green, 81 medium-green, and 39 visually mature) and 5,151 fruit boxes after two reviewers completed exhaustive full-image correction, including faint, blurred, occluded, and boundary-truncated targets. Reviewer task ranges were disjoint and were not used to estimate inter-rater agreement. Reference correction began from a fixed prediction display; confidence-0.25 precision on this set is consequently an anchored diagnostic. Split ledgers, hashes, and protected-set overlap checks are retained in the reproducibility archive.

For external validation, we used the CC BY 4.0 plum images associated with YOLO-plum [14, 15]. The article describes 1,219 screened images, whereas the deposited archive available to us contained 280 JPEG files, 1,536 XML files, and 280 matching image–annotation pairs. Only those 280 auditable pairs were used. We split them by capture-time group and camera model into 193 training, 32 validation, and 55 test images; the external test set contained 1,788 human-annotated fruit. Adjacent burst frames could not cross splits. This local archive discrepancy is reported because silently treating unmatched XML files as images would overstate the usable sample size.

We specified the CitDet external-localization protocol before downloading or inspecting its images and before generating predictions. CitDet contains high-resolution citrus orchard images with human boxes for fruit on trees and on the ground [16]. We used the complete official test archive (119 images; 10,082 boxes: 6,263 ground-fruit and 3,819 tree-fruit boxes), merged both categories into `fruit`, and used no CitDet training data. The official MD5 was verified, and SHA-256 plus perceptual-hash comparison against 41,296 prior project images found no duplicate or near-duplicate. Three frozen inference modes were evaluated with no retraining or parameter selection: direct 1,024-pixel inference (D0), validation-frozen G0, and MARS. Primary endpoints were overall and COCO-small AP50, AP50–95, and AR50; operating-point analyses used confidence 0.25. Every mode retained at most 500 detections per image. Paired differences used 2,000 source-image cluster bootstrap replicates. This test measures generic localization and image-domain size transportability to one citrus archive; stage classification was outside its scope.

For scale analysis, bounding-box area was normalized by source-image area and projected to a 1024 × 1024 reference canvas. Following the COCO convention [17], small objects had reference area below 32² pixels, medium objects were 32² to below 96², and large objects were at least 96². Small fruit represented 36.9% of internal and 46.8% of external plum test objects; the cross-stage reference was still more demanding, with 3,194 of 5,151 fruit in the small-object bin.

The stage dataset used unique source images rather than repeated detector entries. After detector-confidence filtering, training contained 18,926 fruit crops from 2,477 source images and validation contained 2,555 crops from 321 images. A frozen manifest audit found no capture group shared across splits, no training/validation path overlap with either human localization test, and no canonical image-ID overlap between the current detector fit ancestry and the protected stage test. Historical metadata marked all 39 visually mature audit images as previously seen; canonical-ID comparison with the current detector ancestry found zero overlap. Final stage evaluation used the 240-image human audit. A deterministic hash rule retained at most 20 verified fruit per image, yielding 2,792 crops: 1,299 small-green, 1,195 medium-green, and 298 visually mature. Eight visually mature images had no reference fruit, leaving 232 fruit-bearing images (including 31 of 39 visually mature images) for ROI-conditional and strict image-stage evaluation.

### Baseline detector and controlled training protocol

The baseline was the medium YOLO26 configuration, the current real-time end-to-end release when the experiment was frozen [18]. We fixed Ultralytics 8.4.115, the model definition, teacher checkpoint, and split hashes before the controlled runs. The teacher used medium-green fruit only and had not consumed protected-test pseudo labels. All ablations shared teacher ancestry, data, image size, epoch budget, optimizer, and augmentation. Models were trained for 10 epochs at 1024 pixels with batch size 4, AdamW, initial learning rate 7 × 10⁻⁴, cosine decay to 0.1 of the initial rate, 2 warm-up epochs, box-loss gain 9, mosaic probability 0.4, and mosaic closure during the final 5 epochs. MixUp was disabled. Geometric augmentation comprised ±3° rotation, 0.10 translation, 0.45 scale, and horizontal flipping with probability 0.5. Validation AP50–95 selected the checkpoint. A thermal governor paused training above 83 °C and resumed at 80 °C; no reported run reached the pause threshold.

For same-task competitive positioning, YOLO11m [39] and RT-DETR-L [40] were trained on the current detector training and validation partitions and evaluated on the same internal and external-plum tests. The comparison used seeds 20260805–20260807, 10 epochs, 1024-pixel inputs, batch size 4, AdamW, the same learning-rate schedule and geometric augmentation, and a common 500-detection limit. YOLO-derived models used non-maximum suppression at IoU 0.70; RT-DETR-L used native top-query postprocessing. Public pretrained checkpoints initialized the comparators, whereas A2 retained its fruit-domain teacher. The benchmark therefore compares task-level systems under a matched training budget, not isolated architecture effects.

We also identified and corrected a reproducibility hazard in Ultralytics 8.4.115. Its loader bound an internal fixed generator seed instead of the requested experiment seed, and newly added P2 and OSSA parameters were constructed before trainer-level seeding. Nominally independent development runs therefore shared a data stream without fully controlled custom-module initialization. We seeded before model construction, bound the loader generator to the requested seed, verified same-seed identity and different-seed divergence for tensors and loader order, and repeated every ablation with seeds 20260805–20260807. Reported three-seed means and sample standard deviations come from this corrected procedure. The earlier frozen A5 checkpoint remains identified separately because it supplied the already validation-locked GLAF deployment analysis.

### Post-review convergence and difficult-target training update

After the primary analysis, a locked convergence and deployment-training protocol refitted A0, A1, A2, and A5 from the same teacher for up to 30 epochs with patience 8. Exact SHA-256 and perceptual-dHash audits removed every protected-test overlap from the corrected reference sources, leaving 159 leakage-safe images. Blurred, faint, partially visible, heavily occluded, and boundary-truncated fruit remained in the generic `fruit` class. Candidate selection used only the unchanged detector validation set: models within 0.005 overall AP50–95 of the best were eligible, COCO-small AP50–95 resolved non-ties, and differences below 0.002 favored the less complex A2. The selected checkpoint and hash were locked before evaluation. This update supplies post-confirmatory deployment evidence because its three evaluation domains were already known (evidence status in Supplementary Table S3); the consumed cross-stage reference was not reused as a test (Fig. S3).

The difficult-target policy deliberately retained defocused, motion-blurred, or faintly ghosted fruit judged to be real; these targets were not discarded as annotation noise or separated into an easier auxiliary class.

### P2 microfruit path and cross-scale capacity-preserving head

The native three-level detector uses P3, P4, and P5 feature maps. PruScope preserves that pretrained path and adds a top-down connection from P3 to a new stride-4 P2 map. A naïve four-level implementation derives shared regression and classification widths from the first input level, causing the narrow P2 map to shrink all branches. CCPH instead derives hidden widths from P3. For the present channel configuration, this retained regression and classification hidden widths of 64 and 256, respectively, whereas the naïve P2 reference reduced them to 32 and 128. The inherited P3–P5 feature pathway and detection-branch weights can therefore be transferred exactly, while P2 receives a new high-resolution branch. The controlled sequence was A0, native baseline; A1, naïve P2; A2, P2 plus CCPH; and A5, P2 plus CCPH and OSSA. P2-only performance was included to distinguish extra resolution from preserved head capacity.

### Orchard scale-selective attention

OSSA operates at P2 and P3. Three depthwise 3 × 3 branches with dilation 1, 2, and 3 provide effective local receptive fields of 3, 5, and 7 pixels. Global pooled features generate per-channel softmax weights across branches in the spirit of selective-kernel modeling [19], and a spatial gate derived from channel mean and maximum maps emphasizes compact fruit-like regions. A grouped 1 × 1 projection returns the fused signal through a learnable residual layer scale initialized at zero. This initialization permits semantic weight transfer without an abrupt feature-distribution shift. Post-training layer-scale audits tested whether the residual path moved away from zero.

### Global–local adaptive fusion

Direct inference processes the full image at 1024 pixels. GLAF additionally covers the source with deterministic 1,536-pixel tiles at 25% overlap and maps tile detections back to full-image coordinates. Detections close to artificial internal tile boundaries receive a reliability that rises linearly from 0.35 to 1.00 over 4% of the shorter tile dimension; detections at true image borders are not penalized. For reference-canvas box area A, a microfruit weight m = sigmoid[(32² − A)/(0.35 × 32²)] assigns local-stream reliability 0.90 + 0.30m and global-stream reliability 1.10 − 0.20m. Global and local boxes are clustered at IoU 0.55; coordinates use confidence × reliability weights and cluster confidence is the reliability-weighted confidence mean [20], preventing repeated tile observations from becoming duplicate counts.

Unconditional tiling was not assumed to be beneficial. A scene gate was selected using only internal and external validation images. Candidate rules used the number of global detections above a gate confidence and their median reference area. The frozen rule invoked GLAF when at least five global detections had confidence ≥0.05 and their median reference area was ≤2,048 pixels. Test images were evaluated once under this fixed rule. Direct and gated results are both reported because the validation-selected gate represents a deployment policy rather than a replacement for standard detector inference.

The cross-stage reference evaluation predated this validation-selected rule and retained its already frozen historical gate: local inference was invoked when at least 60 full-image boxes were present. This density-60 rule fired on 41 of 240 images (17.1%). We did not regenerate the frozen prediction set with the later validation-selected gate because doing so after reference completion would change the evaluated operating point. The two gates and their checkpoint lineages are therefore reported separately.

### Post-audit microfruit recall analysis

The cross-stage audit motivated an exploratory test of higher-magnification inference. A fixed task-order prefix formed a 160-image development subset and the remaining 80 images formed a one-time confirmation subset. Aggregate audit results were already known, so this experiment is hypothesis-generating (Supplementary Table S3). The frozen A5 checkpoint and postprocessing were retained. Among six protocol-fixed candidates, the selected Microfruit Adaptive Recall Stream (MARS) used 768-pixel tiles at 25% overlap and a scale gate for local-only proposals. The selection rule and full parameter grid are given in Supplementary Methods S1. The chosen configuration was locked before confirmation, and paired differences used 2,000 source-image cluster bootstrap replicates.

### Post-review density-aware microfruit refinement tail

MARS recovered candidates more readily than it localized and ranked them. DART was designed to address that gap with a crop encoder and proposal metadata rather than another shallow detector head. It used the leakage-safe difficult-target A2 checkpoint, one global view, and scale-gated local tiles. The validation-frozen configuration preserved every global prediction unchanged and allowed DART to add only local-only proposal clusters; learned box offsets were disabled in the main configuration and the uncertainty penalty was 0. DART is a post-review descriptive analysis because all three evaluation domains had already been inspected (Supplementary Table S3). Training composition, feature definitions, ablations, and the finite validation grid are reported in Supplementary Methods S2.

### Developmental Continuum Ordinal Head

DCOH treats the three stages as ordered ranks using a rank-consistent cumulative formulation [21]. A ResNet-18 visual encoder [22] transforms each 224 × 224 fruit crop into a feature vector. Three standardized geometry covariates—log normalized width, height, and area—are concatenated with the visual representation. A shared latent stage score is compared with two strictly increasing learned cutpoints. The cumulative probabilities are

`q1 = P(stage > small green)` and `q2 = P(stage > medium green)`, with `q1 ≥ q2` by construction.

Stage probabilities are `(1 − q1, q1 − q2, q2)`, and `q1 + q2` is the 0–2 developmental index. Training minimized binary cumulative-link loss with inverse-frequency crop sampling. The model was optimized for 25 epochs with AdamW, visual-backbone learning rate 2 × 10⁻⁴, head learning rate 10⁻³, weight decay 5 × 10⁻⁴, cosine decay, batch size 64, and early stopping patience 6. Image probabilities were means across fruit crops from the same source image. Strict detector-to-stage evaluation applied the frozen A5 operating point at confidence 0.25, classified retained detections with unchanged DCOH, and matched detections to reference boxes at IoU 0.50. Image coverage, accuracy, and macro F1 use the 232 images containing at least one reference fruit; an image with no retained detection is an error. The originally coded all-240 estimate is retained as a conservative sensitivity analysis because stage correctness is undefined for eight images without reference fruit. Matched-fruit stage accuracy and joint correctly staged fruit recall use fruit-level references. Intervals use 5,000 source-image bootstrap replicates.

We trained visual-plus-geometry, visual-only, and geometry-only controls. To probe acquisition-domain dependence, we also evaluated paired counterfactual inputs: grayscale crops, center-only fruit content, and background-only content. These transformations do not represent natural deployment domains; they test whether performance can survive removal of specific information. Macro F1, balanced accuracy, ordinal mean absolute error, and quadratic-weighted kappa [23] were computed at crop and image levels. Because the deterministic test manifest retained at most 20 fruit crops per image, a post-review sensitivity analysis recomputed image-level probabilities from the first 5, 10, or 20 crops in the immutable crop order; it did not select a deployment crop budget.

### Detection, counting, uncertainty, and computational evaluation

Standard localization metrics were precision, recall, AP50, and AP50–95. A custom COCO-style evaluator applied confidence 0.001, nonmaximum-suppression IoU 0.70, and at most 500 detections per image, and saved predictions in chunks of four images to avoid memory growth and to keep identical rectangular-padding behavior across models. Size-specific AP and average recall were computed from the projected reference areas above.

The confidence used for operational fruit counts was reselected for the validation-locked human-enhanced A2 detector on the two validation sources by minimizing the macro mean relative absolute count error. The frozen threshold was 0.50. Test endpoints were mean absolute error (MAE), root mean square error, bias, coefficient of determination, and Pearson correlation. Count intervals used 5,000 image bootstrap replicates.

Architectural comparisons used 2,000 paired source-image bootstrap replicates, with complete ranked prediction sets recomputed inside each replicate. DCOH's principal interval analysis also used 2,000 source-image replicates; diagnostic controls used 1,000, and count and end-to-end intervals used 5,000. We report two-sided percentile 95% confidence intervals and the bootstrap probability of a positive difference. These intervals describe source-image sampling conditional on fitted checkpoints; training-seed variation is reported separately. No multiplicity adjustment was applied. The A1-versus-A2 capacity-preservation contrast and protocol-locked CitDet endpoints were primary within their analyses. Exact 1.000 percentile bounds in near-ceiling controls arise from finite resampling. Direct-model latency was remeasured for all six reported architectures on the same 64 cached images, at batch 1 and 1024 pixels, after three complete warm-up passes; means and sample standard deviations use ten synchronized full-set passes on one RTX 3090.

### Software and hardware

Training and GPU evaluation were performed under native Windows on two NVIDIA RTX 3090 cards with 24 GB memory each. Parallel jobs were independent single-GPU processes assigned to physical GPU0 or GPU1; no reported run used DDP, NCCL, model parallelism, or inter-GPU tensor communication. Each individual checkpoint was therefore trained on one card, while independent training or inference jobs could run concurrently. The software environment was isolated in the project conda prefix and used Python 3.11, PyTorch, torchvision, Ultralytics, OpenCV, NumPy, pandas, and scikit-learn. CPU jobs used at most eight local threads. Exact package versions, commands, split manifests, model definitions, prediction files, and checksums are included in the reproducibility archive. Numerical results, narrative text, captions, and tables were regenerated from versioned analysis outputs and checked against the machine-readable evaluation reports.

## Results

### The plum cohorts span severe field conditions and a wide apparent-scale continuum

The three cohorts covered foliage-colored microfruit, expanded green fruit, and color-developed mature fruit under uncontrolled cameras and orchard structure. The 240-image cross-stage reference included severe backlighting, deep shadow, leaf occlusion, defocus, boundary truncation, and canopies with more than 200 visible fruit. Figure 2 shows eight fixed adverse-scene examples with human boxes, operating-point detections, and confidence-weighted center-density maps. Small, faint targets disappear first under occlusion and branch clutter. All localization estimates below use the complete human-annotated tests.

### CCPH restores the capacity lost after P2 insertion; OSSA remains domain-dependent

The naïve P2 model A1 underperformed the native A0 detector in both human test domains. CCPH recovered this loss in every controlled run. Internal mean ± sample SD overall/small AP50–95 increased from 0.5822 ± 0.0030/0.3926 ± 0.0053 for A1 to 0.6076 ± 0.0023/0.4326 ± 0.0121 for A2; external values increased from 0.5390 ± 0.0040/0.3286 ± 0.0086 to 0.5692 ± 0.0067/0.3742 ± 0.0101. Mean A2-minus-A1 effects were +0.0255 and +0.0400 internally and +0.0301 and +0.0456 externally. All 12 seed × domain × size cells were positive. The four seed-20260805 source-image bootstrap intervals also excluded zero: +0.0153 to +0.0328, +0.0299 to +0.0742, +0.0233 to +0.0484, and +0.0385 to +0.0743 (Fig. 3; Table 1).

A2 and native A0 were effectively tied: A2-minus-A0 mean effects were −0.0008, −0.0022, −0.0028, and −0.0030 for internal overall, internal small, external overall, and external small AP50–95. CCPH's demonstrated value is the recovery of capacity after P2 insertion, not a claim that A2 surpassed the native detector. The clean OSSA contrast was A5 minus A2. Across three seeds, its mean effects were +0.0005, +0.0081, +0.0047, and +0.0048 in the same four strata; only internal small-object AP50–95 was positive in all three seeds. Corrected seed-20260805 paired bootstraps likewise differed by domain (Fig. 3c). OSSA was retained as a tested lineage, but the data did not support it as a stable cross-domain improvement.

### Same-budget mainstream comparisons test competitive positioning

Across three seeds, internal overall/small AP50–95 was 0.603 ± 0.005/0.412 ± 0.012 for YOLO11m, 0.581 ± 0.008/0.390 ± 0.014 for RT-DETR-L, and 0.608 ± 0.002/0.433 ± 0.012 for PruScope A2. External-plum values were 0.572 ± 0.004/0.360 ± 0.012, 0.480 ± 0.006/0.221 ± 0.022, and 0.569 ± 0.007/0.374 ± 0.010. Under the standardized 64-image benchmark, batch-1 latency was 10.24, 14.57, and 14.23 ms/image, respectively. PruScope A2 gave the highest small-object mean in both plum domains while remaining within real-time detector latency (Fig. 6a, b; Table 2; Table 3). Because initialization differed, this is a task-level comparison.

### Extended convergence and difficult-box human enhancement yield bounded deployment gains

Longer fitting changed architectures differently. Relative to their 10-epoch seed-20260805 runs, the 30-epoch A2 checkpoint changed internal overall/small AP50–95 by +0.0036/−0.0044 and external overall/small AP50–95 by +0.0296/+0.0270. A0 decreased in all four strata, A1 improved in all four, and A5 was nearly unchanged internally but improved externally. This convergence analysis diagnoses optimization sensitivity (Table S3).

On the unchanged validation partition, the leakage-safe difficult-target A2 candidate reached overall/small AP50–95 of 0.5878/0.2809, compared with 0.5784/0.2774 for A5, and was locked before test replay. Relative to the same-architecture 30-epoch reference, small-object AP50–95 changed by +0.0020 internally, +0.0042 on external plum, and +0.0039 on CitDet. The 95% paired intervals were −0.0152 to +0.0200, −0.0039 to +0.0144, and +0.0001 to +0.0072. The meticulous additions broadened the supervised target definition to include faint, defocused, occluded, and truncated fruit; their measured gains were bounded (Fig. S3). The complete-reference deployment fit is kept separate because it consumes protected images.

### Validation-gated local inference targets dense microfruit scenes

For the original deployment checkpoint, validation-gated GLAF raised external overall AP50–95 from 0.5687 to 0.6006 and external small-object AP50–95 from 0.3765 to 0.4407. Internal small-object AP50–95 was 0.4309 with direct inference and 0.4301 with GLAF, a difference well inside the observed 0.0125 cross-job evaluator range. The external gain, coupled with no resolved internal benefit, supports selective instead of unconditional tiling. The gate fired on 71 of 118 internal images and 22 of 55 external images; mean end-to-end latency was 80.7 and 74.8 ms/image.

### A frozen cross-stage human audit exposes the remaining microfruit recall gap

The 240-image human audit contained 5,151 verified fruit. The frozen deployment predictions achieved AP50 0.622, AP50–95 0.498, and AR50 0.708. AP50–95 was 0.376 for 3,194 small image-domain objects, 0.686 for 1,621 medium objects, and 0.732 for 336 large objects. COCO-small fruit in the small-green cohort were hardest (n = 1,293; AP50–95 0.205; AR50 0.340). Cohort-level AP50–95 was 0.366 for small-green, 0.593 for medium-green, and 0.549 for visually mature fruit.

At confidence 0.25 and IoU 0.50, recall was 0.461. It fell from 0.565 for clear fruit to 0.234 for partially visible and 0.070 for heavily occluded fruit. Lowering the prediction floor to 0.001 raised these values to 0.772, 0.612, and 0.406, showing that threshold adjustment alone could not recover the missing targets. Small-green recall was 0.319, compared with 0.552 for medium-green and 0.540 for mature fruit. The remaining detector problem is sharply localized: tiny, foliage-colored, partly visible fruit.

### Higher-magnification tiling recovers microfruit candidates but not localization quality

On the 160-image development subset, MARS increased small-green COCO-small AP50–95 from 0.199 to 0.318 and overall AP50–95 from 0.458 to 0.492. Operating-point precision was 0.884, and latency rose from 35.2 to 278.4 ms/image. The selected configuration maximized the locked microfruit endpoint among eligible candidates; the full grid is shown in Fig. S1 and Supplementary Methods S1.

On the 80-image confirmation subset, MARS raised overall AR50 from 0.757 to 0.895 (difference +0.138; 95% CI +0.088 to +0.195). Among 36 small-green images, COCO-small AR50 rose from 0.355 to 0.759 (+0.404; +0.280 to +0.521). Precise localization did not follow: small-green COCO-small AP50–95 changed from 0.223 to 0.260 (+0.037; −0.051 to +0.122), while overall AP50–95 changed from 0.591 to 0.577. Count MAE improved from 9.83 to 8.53 fruit/image, precision decreased from 0.962 to 0.883, and latency increased from 46.1 to 291.9 ms/image. MARS expands the candidate set; it does not resolve ranking and box quality.

### A zero-tuning public citrus test confirms small-fruit transportability

CitDet contained 10,082 human boxes; 9,638 were COCO-small on the 1,024-pixel reference canvas. With no CitDet training or tuning, direct inference achieved overall AP50 0.668, AP50–95 0.266, and AR50 0.794. Corresponding COCO-small values were 0.660, 0.256, and 0.793. The transfer across species and acquisition archives was strong at IoU 0.50. Stricter localization remained harder (Fig. S2a).

G0 raised COCO-small AP50 to 0.680 and AR50 to 0.828, with paired gains of +0.020 (+0.013 to +0.029) and +0.035 (+0.026 to +0.045). AP50–95 decreased to 0.250 (−0.006; −0.011 to −0.001). MARS reached AP50 0.660, AP50–95 0.246, and AR50 0.824. Direct/G0/MARS latency was 35.2/192.9/656.9 ms/image. The test confirms zero-tuning generic-fruit transportability and a bounded G0 recall benefit; it also reproduces the localization-quality cost of aggressive magnification.

### DART provides a validation-frozen high-recall offline mode

Within the DART single-image global/local pipeline, its matched direct A2 baseline had overall/small AP50–95 of 0.5878/0.2774. The selected tail gave 0.5857/0.2776 and increased small-object AR50 from 0.8526 to 0.9313. This 0.2774 paired baseline differs from the 0.2809 batched architecture-selection estimate because the two inference paths create different letterbox batch shapes; both use the same A2 checkpoint. On the internal, external-plum, and CitDet tests, DART-minus-direct small-object AP50–95 was −0.0020 (95% CI −0.0031 to −0.0011), +0.0057 (−0.0006 to +0.0098), and +0.0004 (−0.0001 to +0.0012).

Recall increased in all three domains: +0.0133 internally (+0.0023 to +0.0264), +0.0382 on external plum (+0.0031 to +0.0607), and +0.0120 on CitDet (+0.0080 to +0.0165). Mean overall AP50–95 across the three domains changed by +0.0002. The RGB-plus-metadata tail outperformed its metadata-only counterpart, whereas an untrained recall stream reduced AP50–95 throughout. On a separate 30-image CitDet efficiency probe, direct A2 required 20.1 ms/image and full DART 864.0 ms/image. The 24-tile recall stream dominated this cost. DART is an offline option for recall-critical analysis; direct A2 remains the balanced mode (Fig. S4; Supplementary Table S2).

### DCOH separates ordered visual cohorts without relying on box scale

Using verified fruit boxes, DCOH achieved accuracy 0.9828, macro F1 0.9786 (95% CI 0.9539–0.9965), and quadratic-weighted κ 0.9696 (0.9286–0.9957) across 232 fruit-bearing images. Small-green, medium-green, and mature recall was 0.992, 0.988, and 0.935. The continuous index clustered near 0, 1, and 2 while retaining within-cohort variation (Fig. 4a, b; Table 4).

Geometry alone yielded macro F1 0.366 and κ 0.156, ruling out box scale as the main shortcut. Visual-only and visual-plus-geometry models both reached image macro F1 0.979; geometry improved crop-level macro F1 from 0.872 to 0.906. Center-only fruit content preserved macro F1 0.975, whereas grayscale reduced it to 0.245. A background-only proxy retained 0.802, indicating residual acquisition context or peripheral fruit pixels. DCOH learned color and appearance effectively, but the intervention also exposed domain cues.

In the strict pipeline, 221 of 232 fruit-bearing images received at least one detection: coverage was 0.953 (95% CI 0.922–0.978), image-stage accuracy 0.944 (0.914–0.970), and macro F1 0.946 (0.908–0.975). The confusion matrix retained every no-detection outcome (Fig. 6c). Seven of the eight zero-reference images correctly produced no detection; one produced two false positives. Under the original all-240 coding, coverage/accuracy/macro F1 was 0.925/0.917/0.915, providing a conservative sensitivity analysis. Among 2,374 matched fruit, stage accuracy was 0.997. Across all 5,151 reference fruit, joint correctly staged recall was 0.459 (0.418–0.498). Stage assignment is not the bottleneck. Localization recall is.

### Validation-calibrated counts transfer imperfectly across domains

At the validation-selected confidence 0.50, the human-enhanced A2 detector reached count MAE 3.60 fruit/image (95% CI 2.70–4.64) internally and 8.11 (4.53–12.69) on external plum. Bias was +1.43 and −5.42 fruit/image, respectively (Supplementary Table S1). The separate frozen cross-stage deployment set, evaluated with the historical density-60 gate at confidence 0.25, had MAE 11.15, bias −10.99, and r = 0.890. Small-green R² was −0.097 despite the strong pooled correlation. Dense microfruit scenes drove the undercount.

### Error analysis identifies density and scale as the primary remaining failure modes

Image-macro detection F1 was 0.774 internally and 0.801 on external plum for the final A2 detector at confidence 0.50. The historical cross-stage prediction set reached 0.595 at confidence 0.25; only 626 of 1,965 small-green fruit were matched. Figure 2 and Figure 5 show the same failure pattern under shadow, defocus, occlusion, and dense branch clutter. False negatives, not stage confusion, determine the present performance ceiling.

## Discussion

PruScope treats localization and ordered stage assignment as linked tasks in one pipeline, while keeping their models separate. A generic fruit detector shares evidence across the three plum cohorts; DCOH operates on the resulting regions. This division is useful when some stages have fewer boxes and when acquisition conditions are correlated with cohort labels. The same-task YOLO11m and RT-DETR-L experiments show that the specialized detector remains competitive beyond its own ablation family.

The CCPH result reveals a practical architectural hazard. In this detector family, adding P2 changes which feature level determines shared head widths. A narrow stride-4 input can therefore shrink the regression and classification branches at every scale. More pixels alone were harmful; preserved semantic capacity restored performance in all 12 controlled cells. A2 then matched, rather than exceeded, the native A0 model. Its purpose is structural: it provides a safe high-resolution lineage on which local inference can be built. OSSA gave a consistent internal small-object mean gain but no stable cross-domain effect. Reporting that result separates a useful negative experiment from the supported CCPH contribution.

Local magnification exposed a second mechanism. It reveals candidates that whole-image resizing suppresses, but those candidates arrive with weaker coordinates, duplicate hypotheses, and poorly calibrated scores. MARS increased AR50 much more than AP50–95. GLAF limited this cost by invoking tiles only when a validation-frozen scale–density rule fired; its external-plum small-object gain was substantial, while the internal difference was unresolved. DART added visual proposal scoring and protected global anchors, producing consistent recall gains at high computational cost. This differs from methods that suppress frequency-domain background noise, stabilize tiny-box regression, or adapt slice counts [36–38]. The strategies are complementary, and their value should be tested prospectively on a new target-species set.

The public CitDet experiment strengthens the localization claim. Zero-tuning inference on 9,638 small citrus fruit reached AP50 0.660 and AR50 0.793. That result is independent of the plum stage task and uses one external archive, yet it shows that the detector learned more than a plum-cohort color rule. The widening gap between AP50 and AP50–95 across local modes points to box quality and ranking as the next small-object problem.

The human audit identifies where that problem matters biologically. Misses accumulated among small-green, partially visible, and dense fruit. These errors propagated directly into count bias and joint stage recall. The classifier itself was highly accurate on matched fruit, and the geometry-only control was weak. DCOH was not reading stage from box area. Its strongest signal came from fruit color and appearance, although grayscale and background interventions detected acquisition-domain dependence. The output is an ordinal index of three operational visual cohorts, not chronological age or physiological maturity.

Together, the pipeline yields four image-derived readouts: fruit locations, visible counts, image-domain apparent-size strata, and an ordered developmental index. The first three describe what is visible from a particular camera view. They are relevant to canopy load mapping and visual harvest scouting, but they are not physical fruit diameter or total yield. Similarly, the mature cohort reflects exterior color development; firmness, soluble solids, and destructive quality measurements were unavailable. Those distinctions make the phenotypes interpretable.

Auditability is also a methodological outcome. The seed-control investigation uncovered a loader behavior that could make nominally independent runs share a data stream and leave custom-module initialization uncontrolled. Binding the requested seed before construction and to the loader generator restored meaningful three-seed comparisons. Source-disjoint splits, protocol locks, checkpoint hashes, paired image bootstraps, and the evidence-status table make the analytical lineage reviewable. The same transparency is applied to model-assisted reference correction: localization recall is valid after exhaustive completion, whereas displayed-prediction precision is treated as diagnostic.

The present data remain cross-sectional and acquisition-confounded. New orchards, cameras, intermediate stages, and a protected target-species test are needed to estimate transportability. Physical maturity measurements would allow the ordinal index to be calibrated against biological traits. Repeated observations would enable temporal association and growth-rate estimation. Until then, the most productive detector endpoint is small-green COCO-small recall on prospectively held-out images, coupled with density-aware count calibration.

## Conclusions

PruScope converts single-view orchard RGB images into fruit locations, visible counts, apparent-size strata, and an ordered visual-stage index with one auditable pipeline. CCPH recovered the accuracy lost when P2 was added in all 12 seed × domain × size cells, and zero-tuning inference transferred to 9,638 small CitDet fruit. DCOH separated the three plum cohorts with reference-ROI macro F1 0.979; in the complete pipeline, fruit-bearing image macro F1 was 0.946 and matched-fruit stage accuracy 0.997. Joint correctly staged fruit recall was 0.459 because microfruit localization remained limiting. Direct A2 is the balanced real-time mode. Validation-gated GLAF and offline DART offer higher-recall alternatives, with DART increasing small-object AR50 across all three evaluated domains at markedly greater latency. The output size strata are image-domain measurements, and the developmental index represents cross-sectional visual cohorts. Longitudinal growth and physiological maturity require new temporal and biological calibration data.

## Supplementary Materials

### Supplementary Methods S1. MARS candidate selection

The post-audit MARS experiment used a fixed task-order split: images T0001–T0160 formed development and T0161–T0240 formed one-time confirmation. Candidate F0 was the balanced direct baseline. Five local-inference candidates varied tile magnification, coverage, and scene gating while retaining the frozen A5 checkpoint and common postprocessing. Eligibility required development overall AP50–95 no more than 0.01 below F0 and precision of at least 0.85 at confidence 0.25. The primary objective was small-green COCO-small AP50–95; ties within 0.002 favored lower latency and then fewer tiles. Candidate F5, subsequently named MARS, used 768-pixel tiles with 25% overlap and invoked the local stream when at least one global detection had confidence ≥0.05 and median 1,024-canvas area ≤4,096 pixels. It was selected before confirmation. Aggregate audit results were already known when this split was created, so its evidence status is exploratory (Table S3).

### Supplementary Methods S2. DART training and frozen validation grid

DART used the leakage-safe difficult-target A2 checkpoint and 159 eligible human-corrected images. The tail received an RGB proposal crop plus frozen metadata describing detector confidence, scale, aspect ratio, image position, tile-edge reliability, global/local observations, cross-view agreement, and coordinate dispersion. A deterministic group-disjoint 80/20 fit/monitor split selected the epoch, after which the tail was refit on all eligible images. The anchor-preserving revision retained every global A2 prediction and permitted only deduplicated local-only additions; learned box offsets were disabled in the primary grid. The finite validation grid crossed score weight {0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1.00}, uncertainty penalty {0, 0.25}, duplicate IoU {0.55, 0.65, 0.75}, and maximum addition area {1,024, 4,096} pixels. Selection maximized small-object AP50–95 subject to overall AP50–95 remaining within 0.005 of the matched direct baseline; ties within 0.002 used small-object AR50 and then latency. The selected setting used score weight 0.25, uncertainty penalty 0, duplicate IoU 0.55, and maximum addition area 4,096 pixels. No protected-test result changed this grid.
